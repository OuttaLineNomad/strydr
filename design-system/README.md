Strydr is a self-hosted running and training dashboard — one person's data, pulled from their own Apple Watch, read back through an AI coach and a set of charts that take the material seriously. Recording itself happens elsewhere (the Watch's own workout app); Strydr never starts, pauses or stops anything — it's purely the analytics, trends and coaching layer on top of data that already exists. It is built from scratch for this project, not extracted from an existing codebase, so this is a first, intentionally small system: the surfaces, the type and color language, and four components that put the material to work. Everything here is meant to be extended.

## Voice

Strydr talks like a coach who respects your time, not a gamified app chasing a streak. State the number, then the one sentence of context that makes it useful — "8:42/mi, 14 sec faster than last Tuesday's easy run" beats "Great job!! 🔥". No exclamation points as a default register; earn them with a real personal record. Second person, direct ("You're on pace for a 24:10 5K"), and comfortable with real units and real numbers rather than vague praise.

## Color & theming

Both themes are real, not a dark mode generated by inverting the light one — every color below is set deliberately per theme and checked at 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text, icons, and the focus ring, against the surface its usage note names.

- **`surface-0` / `surface-1` / `surface-2`** — page, card, and raised-sheet backgrounds. In dark mode these stay a true near-black charcoal rather than navy — the glass highlight is what gives dark mode its light, not a blue-tinted base.
- **`ink` / `ink-secondary` / `ink-muted`** — the three text weights. `ink-muted` is for 13px-and-up captions only; never drop body copy to it.
- **`accent`** (coral) is the one brand color used for fills and icons 24px and larger — the primary button, the active nav icon, the logo. It is too light to pass small-text contrast on its own, so text and icons under 24px use **`accent-text`**, a darker coral that reads the same but clears 4.5:1. The same split exists for **`secondary`** (cyan — maps, routes, GPS) and **`tertiary`** (gold — personal records, streaks), each with its own `-text` sibling. Where any of these sits as a *fill*, the text on top of it is **`ink-on-*`** — dark ink, not white; at this lightness dark text reads better in both themes, which is also why a brightened dark-mode accent still takes dark text rather than flipping to white.
- **`success`** and **`danger`** cover trend and status. Both are required to ship with an icon or a word, never color alone — `success`/`danger` sit close enough in lightness to `tertiary`'s gold and to each other that hue alone isn't a safe signal for every runner's color vision.
- **`focus-ring`** (shared with `accent-text`) and **`shadow-focus`** mark every focusable element — never remove a focus outline for being "too bold" against the glass.

## Glass

Strydr's one material, built to match Apple's actual Liquid Glass (WWDC 2025 / iOS 26), not the frosted-card look that usually gets called "glassmorphism." Apple's own description: a translucent material that *reflects and refracts* its surroundings, with specular highlights that shift in real time as things move, adapting between light and dark environments. Three things carry that at rest, all in the Materials card:

- **A glint**, not a wash — a small, bright point of light baked into the panel's own background at the corner nearest an implied light source (`glass-specular`, top-left, consistently — never re-angled per instance). The earlier version of this system used a diagonal gradient sheen across the whole panel; that reads as a coating, not a lens.
- **A two-tone bevel** — `shadow-glass` carries a bright inset top edge and a pooled dark inset bottom edge (`glass-lens-dark`), standing in for a real lens rim bending light toward the source on one side and away on the other.
- **`backdrop-filter: blur() saturate(180%) contrast(1.05)`** — the contrast bump is what makes the material *concentrate* what's behind it rather than just softening it, closer to Apple's "reflects and refracts" than a plain blur.

That recipe (`.glass`, weighted `glass--sm` / `glass--lg` / default `glass`) is what every card, sheet, bar and glass button uses, and it's deliberately cheap — real-time refraction is GPU-native on Apple's platforms and not something CSS can fully replicate without cost. For the one place fidelity matters more than performance — a hero or onboarding moment — the Materials card also documents **true SVG-displacement lensing** (`backdrop-filter: url(#glass-lens) blur() saturate()`, an `feTurbulence` + `feDisplacementMap` chain): the background genuinely bends behind the panel rather than just blurring. It renders reliably in Chromium, is unreliable in Safari, and is expensive — one surface per screen, never the whole dashboard.

Three weights only — `blur-sm` (chips, the tab bar), `blur-md` (the default: cards, chart containers), `blur-lg` (sheets, full overlays). Glass needs something behind it with actual color variation to read as glass at all — a flat `surface-0` behind a flat panel just looks like a slightly-transparent gray box. Dashboard backgrounds carry a soft, fixed color wash (see the Materials and Button cards); a map screen's glass sits over the map itself and needs no added wash.

**Where Strydr intentionally diverges from Apple's own rule:** the Human Interface Guidelines reserve Liquid Glass for one functional layer — controls and navigation (tab bars, sidebars, toolbars) that float *above* content — and explicitly keep it off the content layer itself (lists, cards, media), with a narrow exception for a transient control like a slider mid-drag. Strydr puts glass on dashboard cards and stat tiles too, which is a deliberate extension for the "everything is glass" sporty feel the brief asked for, not an oversight. Keep that choice in mind if this system ever needs to feel more restrained — the fix is cards back to a solid `surface-1`/`surface-2`, glass reserved for the tab bar and sheets, not a change to the material itself.

This system has since been checked against outside dashboard critique too, separate from Apple's own rules — most of that critique treats any shadow or glow on a card at rest, glass included, as a sign of a dashboard nobody really designed. Strydr keeps the shadow anyway, for the same reason it keeps glass on cards: it's load-bearing for the one material this whole system is named for, not a decorative add-on a generic card picked up by default. See "Checked against outside critique" under Charts & data for where that same critique *did* change something.

See the Motion card for how glass moves — it isn't static.

## Typography

Three families, used by role, not interchangeably: **`display`** (Space Grotesk) for headlines and every big number — it's geometric enough to read as "data," not "marketing." **`body`** (Sora) for every sentence of UI text. **`mono`** (JetBrains Mono) for anything meant to align in a column — split tables, lap times, raw GPS coordinates. Load all three from Google Fonts; none are self-hosted in this system yet.

`Display` and `Metric` share the display family at different jobs: `Display` styles are headlines and section titles; `Metric` styles (`metric-hero`, `metric-lg`, `metric-sm`) are specifically for numbers that update live, always set with `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` so a changing value doesn't reflow its neighbors. `label` is the only style with letter-spacing — it's for short, all-caps-adjacent UI words (button labels, tab labels), never for a sentence.

## Spacing & radius

An 8-point-adjacent scale (`space-1`–`space-10`, 4px to 40px) and five radii from `radius-sm` (10px, chips) to `radius-full` (pills, the tab bar, avatars). There is no sharp corner anywhere in Strydr — the smallest radius is still a visibly rounded 10px. `radius-lg` (24px) is the default for anything that would be called a "card."

## Charts & data

Three color roles, not one, picked by the job the chart is doing — never reach for whichever token happens to be closest.

- **Categorical** (identity — telling series apart): `chart-elevation`, `chart-pace`, `chart-power`, `chart-cadence`, `chart-heart-rate`, `chart-speed`, in that fixed order — validated for lightness, chroma, and color-vision-deficiency separation against both surfaces; don't reassign them per chart or cycle them for a metric outside this list. They're deliberately a shade off the brand `accent`/`secondary`/`tertiary` tokens: those are tuned for small-text contrast, chart marks are tuned for mark-on-surface contrast and telling six lines apart, and the two jobs don't share one set of hex values well. Three of the six (`chart-power`, `chart-cadence`, `chart-heart-rate`) sit under 3:1 against the light surface by design — give those lines a visible label or the table view rather than relying on the line's own contrast.
- **Sequential** (magnitude — one series, many intensities): `heat-1`–`heat-5`, one hue, light to dark. Used by the Heatmap card; reach for this any time position can't carry the value and color has to (a calendar grid, a pace-by-segment map overlay), never for telling multiple series apart.
- **De-emphasis** (`chart-other`) and **chart furniture** (`chart-grid` for gridlines, `chart-axis` for the baseline): grayscale, recessive on purpose, not held to the same contrast floor as a data mark. `chart-other` is what the **emphasis** form uses for every series except the one that's the point — see Line Chart.

One rule regardless of role: **a chart with two or more series always carries a legend**; up to four are also direct-labeled, so identity is never color-alone. **Never give a chart two y-axes** — two measures of different scale are two charts, small multiples, or indexed to a common base. Every chart in this system ships a "View as table" twin built from the same data (Line Chart, Bar Chart, Heatmap all do this the same way) — that's not optional polish, it's the accessible equivalent WCAG requires, and it's also where a value goes when there's no room to show it directly.

**Not yet in this system:** a heart-rate-zone ramp (five zones, recovery to max). It wants its own validated set rather than a guess — ask once there's a real zone model to tune against.

**Checked against outside dashboard critique, and kept what held up.** A left-aligned numbers column, a decorative sparkline parked beside a KPI, and a caption that only restates axis units are three of the most common tells of a dashboard nobody really looked at — this system now avoids all three: `chart-table` right-aligns every column but the first (the day or week label stays prose-left, every value column reads as a stacked column of digits); Stat Tile dropped the sparkline it used to draw next to the hero number, since Line Chart and Bar Chart already own the honest version of that trend; Line Chart's caption states the week's actual fastest day instead of just naming the chart's units. One rule turned out to already be satisfied: pills were always named `success`/`danger` for the outcome, never `up`/`down` for the direction, which is exactly the "color says good or bad, not which way the number moved" rule that critique argues for — worth stating here so a future metric with an inverted "good" direction (pace, say — slower is the bad direction even though the number goes up) doesn't get its pill wired to raw sign by habit. See Stat Tile's card for the pill-color and zero-change rules in full, and the Glass section above for where this same critique's advice was knowingly *not* taken.

## Iconography

A custom thirteen-glyph set — see the Icons card — rather than a named external library, so every icon shares the system's own stroke weight and corner language exactly. One rule throughout: 24×24 viewBox, 1.8px stroke, round caps and joins, no fill (the dashboard bars and the speed gauge's pivot dot are the only two exceptions, both explained on the card). Icons are documented as copyable inline markup, not asset uploads — an uploaded SVG renders through `<img>` and can't take `currentColor`, so it could never pick up the active-tab coral or dim for a disabled state.

## Logo

A wordmark (`Strydr` set in the display typeface) and a standalone symbol mark — a single coral stride-curve, drawn from the same arc language as the cover's pattern — under `assets/Logo/`: `strydr-mark.svg` (the symbol alone), `strydr-app-icon.svg` (the mark on a coral tile, for a home-screen icon), `strydr-wordmark.svg` / `strydr-wordmark-dark.svg` (text only, for light/dark surfaces), and `strydr-lockup.svg` / `strydr-lockup-dark.svg` (mark and wordmark together). Unlike the icon set, these are real static artwork — a logo's color is fixed, not themed — so they're uploaded assets, not inline markup. Never recolor, stretch, or redraw the mark; use the dark-surface variants rather than placing the light-surface wordmark on a dark background.

## Components (intentional additions)

Nine, chosen because they're what the brief's own description — glass, bright metrics, a bottom-nav app, real charts — needed to become concrete rather than theoretical: **Glass Panel** (the material itself, including the true-lensing technique), **Motion** (how glass moves — morph and scroll-responsive chrome), **Button** (primary / glass / secondary / icon), **Stat Tile** (the dashboard's basic unit), **Tab Bar** (navigation — four flat destinations, no recording action), **Icons** (the thirteen-glyph set, documented as copyable markup), **Line Chart** (time series, the emphasis form, with hover and a table-view twin), **Bar Chart** (single series, direct-labeled), **Heatmap** (the sequential ramp, a 13-week training-load calendar). Nothing else — no form inputs, no modals, no toast, no scatter or stacked-bar chart yet — has been designed; add them as real screens need them rather than guessing a full kit now.

## Motion

Two curves, not one — see the Motion card for both live (a morphing search control and a scroll-responsive tab bar). They're set as plain CSS custom properties in `bundle.css` (not a `tokens.json` category; this system's token grammar doesn't have a motion type), but they're as load-bearing as any color:

- **`--ease-glass`** — `cubic-bezier(.34, 1.56, .64, 1)`. A small, controlled overshoot. Apple's own language for Liquid Glass is "gel-like," and real Liquid Glass bounces on press and on morph — `.interactive()` scales down with a bounce and a shimmer, and shape changes animate on a `.bouncy` spring. This curve is for **glass only**: a button or tab press (`scale(.965)` on `:active`), a card settling after release, a control morphing into another shape (the search field in the Motion card).
- **`--ease-settle`** — `cubic-bezier(.22, 1, .36, 1)`. No overshoot. Everything that isn't glass moving — a sheet opening, a tab switching, the scroll-responsive bar resizing — settles on this curve instead. Mixing the two up is what makes a UI read as either dead (all settle) or cartoonish (all bounce); glass bounces, the UI built from it stays calm.

Durations: `--dur-press` (120ms, scale-down and touch-glow-in on contact), `--dur-release` (320ms, settling back out), `--dur-morph` (420ms, a control growing or shrinking into another shape).

**Touch illumination** — Apple's glass lights up where you actually touch it, not uniformly. `.glass-interactive` pairs with a `.glass-touch` child element; a pointer handler sets `--tx`/`--ty` to the contact point and the element fades in a radial `glass-specular` glow centered there, then fades out on release. See the Materials card's press demo.

**Reduce Motion** drops every duration above to near-zero and removes the overshoot — glass still changes state, it just cuts instead of bouncing (handled globally in `bundle.css` via `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`, no per-component work needed). **Reduce Transparency** and **Increase Contrast** are handled in the material itself, not here — see the Glass section.

## Dark mode

Both themes ship, and the glass material carries over on purpose: dark mode keeps a *light*-tinted translucency and highlight (not an inverted dark sheen), because that's what keeps a frosted panel reading as glass rather than as a plain dark card with a border. Brand hues brighten slightly in dark mode (`accent` goes from `#ff5a36` to `#ff6b4d`, for instance) to hold their contrast against the darker surfaces without looking washed out.
